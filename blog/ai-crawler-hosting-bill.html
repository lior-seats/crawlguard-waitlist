Is an AI Crawler Spiking Your Hosting Bill? How to Diagnose and Stop It

September 2026 · 10 min read · Check your site's exposure free →

You open your Vercel dashboard on a Monday morning and see your bandwidth usage has tripled. Or your AWS bill jumped $200 compared to last month. Your traffic looks normal. No deployment changes. No viral post.

There's a very good chance an AI crawler is the culprit.

Since 2023, AI companies have been aggressively crawling the web to build training datasets for their models. The bots — GPTBot (OpenAI), ClaudeBot (Anthropic), Bytespider (ByteDance), meta-externalagent (Meta), and others — don't show up in your analytics because they're not browsers. But they absolutely show up in your bandwidth bill.

Step 1: Confirm it's a bot, not a traffic spike

The first step is ruling out legitimate traffic. Signs that point to a bot rather than a real traffic spike:

  • Bandwidth increased but pageviews in Google Analytics / Plausible stayed flat
  • High bandwidth to pages that normally get little traffic
  • Requests hitting old or obscure URLs across your whole site (crawlers tend to follow every link)
  • Traffic from data center IP ranges rather than residential/mobile IPs
  • Requests at unusual hours with no geographic correlation to your user base

Step 2: Find the culprit in your server logs

Every major hosting platform gives you access to raw access logs. Look for the User-Agent field — that's how you identify the bot.

1 Vercel: Go to your project → Analytics → check if Vercel Analytics is enabled. For raw logs, you need Vercel Log Drain (Enterprise feature) or check your _logs endpoint if configured.

Alternative: Check your Vercel usage dashboard for unusual bandwidth spikes by day — this narrows the timeframe before you dig into logs.

2 Cloudflare: Go to Analytics → Traffic. Under "Bots," you'll see automated traffic. For User-Agent breakdown, use the Firewall → Events log or Workers Analytics if you have a Worker in front of your origin.

3 AWS / nginx / Apache: Search your access logs for known AI bot User-Agents:

# Find AI crawler requests in nginx/Apache access logs
grep -E "GPTBot|ClaudeBot|anthropic-ai|Bytespider|meta-externalagent|PerplexityBot|CCBot" /var/log/nginx/access.log | wc -l

# Show top bots by request count
grep -oP '"[^"]*(?:GPTBot|ClaudeBot|Bytespider|meta-externalagent|PerplexityBot|CCBot)[^"]*"' /var/log/nginx/access.log | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn | head -20

# Estimate bandwidth by bot (requires %O in log format)
awk '/Bytespider/{sum += $10} END {print sum/1024/1024 " MB"}' /var/log/nginx/access.log

4 Google Cloud / Firebase Hosting: Use Cloud Logging with a filter:

resource.type="http_load_balancer"
httpRequest.userAgent:"GPTBot" OR httpRequest.userAgent:"Bytespider"

💡 Shortcut: Check your robots.txt access first

Compliant bots fetch your robots.txt before crawling. A spike in hits to /robots.txt — especially from data center IPs — is a strong signal that crawlers are active on your site. Check your access logs for GET /robots.txt entries.

Once you know which bot is responsible, you can estimate cost. Here's the math for common platforms:

🚨 Real developer reports

On HN and Reddit, developers have reported AI crawler bandwidth bills ranging from $50 to $1,200+ per month on platforms like Vercel, Netlify, and AWS. One common pattern: a SaaS with <1,000 active users discovering their Vercel bill jumped 8x due to Bytespider crawling their entire site every few days.

Step 4: Stop the bleeding

Once you've identified the bot, you have three options in order of effectiveness:

Option A: Update robots.txt (fastest, works for compliant bots)

# Add to your robots.txt
User-agent: Bytespider
Disallow: /

User-agent: GPTBot
Disallow: /

User-agent: CCBot
Disallow: /

For a complete list with all AI crawlers, see our full robots.txt blocking guide.

Option B: Cloudflare WAF rule (enforced, no trust in compliance)

# Cloudflare → Security → WAF → Custom Rules
# Expression: (http.user_agent contains "Bytespider") or (http.user_agent contains "GPTBot")
# Action: Block
# This is enforced at the edge — the bot never reaches your origin or consumes your bandwidth

Option C: nginx/Apache block (origin-level enforcement)

# nginx — add to your server {} block
if ($http_user_agent ~* "(Bytespider|GPTBot|ClaudeBot|meta-externalagent|CCBot)") {
    return 403;
}

⚠️ Cloudflare caching won't help here

If you're already on Cloudflare, you might assume cached pages don't cost bandwidth. But if Cloudflare's cache is cold (or the bot bypasses cache with unique query parameters), requests still hit your origin. The fix is a WAF rule that blocks the bot before it reaches your cache or origin.

Step 5: Monitor so it doesn't happen again
